How it works

What we do, in 4 steps

  1. Get the measurements. Monthly values since 1981 for each place.
  2. Take out the seasons. Florida is always hotter in August than in January. That's not a trend, so we compare each month only with the same month in other years (this July vs. a normal July).
  3. Look for a steady change. We draw the trend line and measure how fast it goes up or down: the speed of change, shown per 10 years.
  4. Ask: could this be luck? Weather goes up and down randomly. A math test (the Mann-Kendall test) checks how likely it is to see a trend this steady if nothing were really changing.

What "How sure are we?" means

AnswerMeaning
Very sureLess than a 5% chance the trend is just luck. Scientists call this statistically significant.
Not sure yetThere's a hint of a change, but luck could still explain it. More years of data may settle it.
No real evidenceThe ups and downs look random.

Where the data comes from

  • Temperature, rain and humidity: NASA's MERRA-2, a reconstruction of the weather built from satellites, weather balloons and ground stations, delivered by NASA POWER. Monthly since 1981.
  • Sea level: NOAA tide gauges along Florida's coast. NASA uses tide gauge records like these to check its satellite measurements of the ocean. See NASA's Sea Level Change portal.

Honest limits

  • We look for straight-line trends. Some real changes curve or come in sudden steps.
  • A trend shows that something changed, not why. The "why" notes are possibilities, not proof.
  • MERRA-2 squares are about 50 km (30 miles) wide, so a place's value covers a whole area, not one street.
  • Tide gauges measure the sea compared with the land, so sinking land also shows up as rising sea.
